## The "Company of One"

The distinction between freelancer and enterprise has blurred. Consultants, developers, designers and creators now operate as professional businesses with real revenue, real clients and real exposure — but often without the legal structure that matches.

The UK answer is a **private limited company with a single director and shareholder**. It costs more to run than a sole trader. It buys you meaningful protection, and in most commercial situations that trade is worth making.

> [!NOTE]
> A "solopreneur" UK Ltd means one person owns 100% of the shares and holds all director roles. It is entirely valid — Companies House has no minimum share count and no restriction on a sole director also being the sole shareholder.

---

## Benefit One: Limited Liability

The core reason sole traders incorporate, and the one that matters most when the arithmetic is done honestly.

As a sole trader, business debts are **your** debts. A £40,000 supplier invoice you cannot pay is a claim against your personal assets — your home, your savings, your car. Personal guarantees on business premises mean the landlord can pursue you directly.

As a sole director of a Ltd, liability is limited to the value of your shareholding. If you hold £1 of shares, that is the most a creditor can generally claim from you in respect of the company's obligations. The company owns the debt; you do not.

**Where this matters in practice:**

- A client disputes an invoice and refuses to pay — the loss is the company's, not yours personally
- An equipment lease or supplier contract goes wrong — limited to the company's assets
- A commercial lease on a personal guarantee — the guarantee is a separate matter and must be handled carefully, but the general trading liabilities are ring-fenced

The protection is real, but it is not absolute. It does not protect you from personal fraud, from giving a personal guarantee, from wrongful trading while insolvent, or from anything you do personally rather than through the company.

## Benefit Two: Tax Efficiency

Corporation Tax on profits starts at **19%**, against higher rates for personal income. The picture changes once you pay yourself a salary or take dividends.

**The typical solopreneur structure:**

1. Pay yourself a modest **salary** through PAYE, up to the National Insurance threshold
2. Retain profits in the company
3. Take additional amounts as **dividends** when needed

Dividends attract no National Insurance. Salary does. The split reduces total cost.

**A worked example** on £60,000 extracted from a newly formed company:

| Approach | Company tax | Your personal tax + NI | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| All as salary | 19% on profit | Salary rates + 8% NI | Highest |
| £12,000 salary + £48,000 dividend | 19% on profit | Lower rate on dividend | Lowest |
| All as dividend | 19% on profit | Lowest rate, no NI | Middle |

The gap between the highest and lowest is typically **£3,000–£6,000 a year** for a solo consultant at this income level.

**R&D tax credits** also apply to one-person companies. A solo developer claiming R&D relief can receive a substantial cash credit from HMRC even while loss-making.

> [!WARNING]
> The salary-and-dividend split is a planning strategy, not a loophole. HMRC operates "always on" checks that flag directors taking dividends while the company cannot pay PAYE or VAT on time. Take dividends only from genuinely accumulated profit.

## Benefit Three: Credibility and Contracting

A limited company is the default legal form in the UK. When a UK client runs a supplier onboarding process, a Ltd passes checks that a sole trader frequently does not.

**Practical consequences:**

- UK public-sector and large-corporate procurement often requires a company
- Professional indemnity insurance is easier to obtain as a Ltd
- Suppliers and subcontractors frequently require a company registration number and VAT number
- A Ltd continues to exist when the individual leaves — contracts and IP stay with the entity

**For non-UK founders** the effect is larger still. A UK Ltd gives you something a foreign sole proprietorship cannot: a UK legal entity that UK clients, payment providers and marketplaces recognise without explanation.

This is often the single strongest commercial argument, more than the liability protection.

## Benefit Four: Bringing in a Partner Later

A sole trader cannot issue shares. Adding a partner means restructuring the entire business, transferring assets, and often re-contracting with every client.

A Ltd solves this in one step:

1. Issue new shares to the incoming partner
2. Update the Confirmation Statement
3. The partner now owns part of the company

This matters for founders who plan to grow. Two people consulting together is not a partnership agreement — it is a company with two shareholders.

**Watch the dilution maths.** Companies House issues 100 shares by default. If you are solo, issue a sensible number to yourself (say 1,000), then allocate a defined proportion to a partner. Registering the **persons with significant control** on Companies House is mandatory — anyone holding more than 25% of shares or voting rights.

## Benefit Five: Asset and IP Ownership

Company assets belong to the company. This matters for:

- **Intellectual property** developed while trading — assigned to the company, not to you personally
- **Business bank accounts** — ring-fenced from personal finances
- **Contract positions** — the company holds the contract, so it survives you taking other work

**IP assignment matters more than most founders realise.** If you wrote software, designed a brand, or developed a process *as a sole trader*, that IP is legally yours personally and does not automatically transfer to a company you form afterwards. Assigning it explicitly is a separate step, and it costs nothing but needs doing.

## When a Sole Trader Is the Better Choice

Being honest about the other side:

**Stay a sole trader if:**

- Your turnover is genuinely low and predictable
- You have no contracts that could generate liability claims
- Your clients only need a bank transfer and an invoice
- You want zero admin overhead
- You are just starting out and validating an idea

The statutory accounts filing, the CS01, the registered office and the Corporation Tax registration all cost time and sometimes money. For a low-turnover solo activity, that overhead is real and the benefits may not justify it.

**Form a Ltd if:** you invoice businesses, you carry meaningful liability exposure, you want to pay yourself more than £50,000, you plan to grow, or you are non-UK based serving UK clients.

- [ ] Decide on share capital and who holds what
